# YatraKavach closes the warning-to-action gap

**Bottom line.** Real early-warning systems (EWS, systems that detect a hazard and warn people before it hits) for landslides and flash floods all use the same pattern. A static susceptibility layer (a map of where the ground is weak) is combined with a dynamic rainfall trigger (a rule such as "24-hour rain above X mm"). The output is a 3–4 colour level. A human forecaster or officer makes the final call. India already has the pieces in silos. IMD runs Doppler radars (radars that measure rain and wind inside clouds) in J&K. GSI issues district landslide bulletins, but no J&K district is on the published list. The Amarnath and Vaishno Devi boards run RFID (radio-frequency ID cards read at gates) and CCTV crowd systems. NDMA's SACHET sends area-wide SMS and cell-broadcast alerts. The three recent yatra disasters (Amarnath, 8 Jul 2022; Chasoti/Machail, 14 Aug 2025; Adhkuwari/Vaishno Devi, 26 Aug 2025) show the same break. A warning existed in some form, but nobody turned it into a timely, owned, segment-level "stop and clear" action that reached the pilgrims. That gap is YatraKavach's niche. The niche is not better weather prediction. It is **route-segment risk × people exposed now → officer-approved, audited, multilingual CAP alert** (CAP = Common Alerting Protocol, the international XML format for public warnings). Hack for Social Cause (HSC) has only one past edition (2026). In it, a J&K disaster project (A.N.T.S, IUST) took 3rd place, and solo entrants took 1st and 2nd. So a solo J&K disaster entry is viable, but it must look clearly different from A.N.T.S. One developer can build a credible prototype in 3 days. Build the segment scoring, the approval workflow, the CAP output and a replay of the August 2025 events for real. Simulate the sensors, the RFID counts and the government channels, and label them as simulated.

A note on deadline (sourced): the official HSC page gives two different registration/submission deadlines, 15 Oct and 27 Oct 2026 ([MY Bharat HSC page](https://mybharat.gov.in/pages/hack-for-social-cause)). ## Every operational EWS pairs a slow map with a fast rain trigger

The core model is an **empirical rainfall threshold** (a rule fitted to past landslides, not to physics). The classic form is the power law I = α·D^β. Here I is mean rain intensity in mm/h and D is duration in hours. Caine (1980) proposed it first (sourced: [Cepeda et al., EGU2009](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2009/EGU2009-12290.pdf)). His global curve is **I = 14.82·D^-0.39** (sourced: [arXiv 1306.1529](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1306.1529)). For the Nepal Himalaya, Dahal and Hasegawa (2008) fitted **I = 73.90·D^-0.79** from 193 landslides (sourced: [dpnet.org.np PDF](https://dpnet.org.np/uploads/files/rainfall%20threshold2008%202021-09-17%2006-39-49.pdf)). Example: for a 6-hour storm, the Nepal curve gives 73.90 × 6^-0.79 ≈ 18 mm/h, or about 108 mm in 6 hours (estimate, own arithmetic).

The only J&K-specific thresholds found come from Shah et al. (2024). They used 220 landslides in the Kashmir valley and 190 on NH-44 (Udhampur–Banihal), from 1990–2021. The daily thresholds are **9.4 mm/day (valley)** and **14.35 mm/day (NH-44)**. The antecedent thresholds (rain that has already soaked the soil over earlier days) are **55 mm over 15 days** and **53 mm over 20 days** (sourced: [Shah et al., Natural Hazards 2024](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/nathaz/v120y2024i2d10.1007_s11069-023-06254-w.html)). These values are low. A single wet monsoon day exceeds them. So use them for the "watch" level only, and use multiples or the Himalayan I–D curves for "warning" (inference).

Bigger systems add soil memory or machine learning. Japan's JMA combines **60-minute rain with a Soil Water Index** (a "tank model": a simple model that treats soil as stacked water tanks that fill and drain). It computes this on a **5 km grid** and shows it at 1 km in some prefectures (sourced: [Geotechnics 2023](https://doaj.org/article/12fa7c063b384fa2bbf9e0c371e6763e); [JMA 2019 notice](https://www.data.jma.go.jp/add/suishin/oshirase/pdf/20190919.pdf)). NASA's **LHASA 2** uses XGBoost (a gradient-boosted decision-tree model). Its inputs are slope, faults, lithology (rock type), soil moisture, snow and IMERG satellite rain. It gives a ~1 km global hazard map (sourced: [NASA GSC-18595-1](https://software.nasa.gov/software/GSC-18595-1); [GitHub nasa/LHASA](https://github.com/nasa/LHASA)). NASA says its values are a **hazard ranking, not calibrated probabilities** (sourced: [LHASA 2.0 README](https://acdisc.gsfc.nasa.gov/data/Landslide/Global_Landslide_Nowcast.2.0.0/doc/README.Global_Landslide_Nowcast_2.0.0.pdf)). Physics models such as TRIGRS compute a factor of safety (resisting force ÷ driving force; below 1 means the slope fails) per pixel. They need soil depth, cohesion and conductivity data (sourced: [UTS repository](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/139458)). Those data do not exist for J&K tracks, so TRIGRS is out of scope for 3 days (inference).

The output side is always a short colour ladder with a human at the end. GSI's LANDSLIP bulletins give **Low → Very High for the next 48 h, per district**, to district administrators (sourced: [LANDSLIP bulletin KP](https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/532238/1/LANDSLIP_KP_LandslideForecastBulletin_3March2022_OR21070.pdf)). Sri Lanka's NBRO uses **24-hour rain of >75 / >100 / >150 mm** for yellow/amber/red (sourced, news: [Newsfirst 2020](https://www.newsfirst.lk/2020/05/15/nbro-issues-early-landslide-warning)). Norway's forecasters read model outputs in a tool called xgeo and then set the level. In 10 years, **92% of warnings were yellow, 7% orange and 1% red** (sourced: [varsom.no](https://www.varsom.no/en/news/general/the-norwegian-landslide-early-warning-service-turns-10-years-old/)). Hong Kong's Landslip Warning is a **joint human decision** by two agencies, fed by more than 120 rain gauges. The agency states openly that it gives false alarms and can miss sudden storms (sourced: [HK Slope Safety](https://ginfo.cedd.gov.hk/hkss/en/protection-against-landslide-emergency/landslip-warning-system/index.html)). Since May 2024 it also issues an area-specific "Special Landslip Advisory". That advisory is the closest real-world model for per-segment warnings (sourced, same link).

Sensor networks exist but do not scale to a whole track. Amrita's Munnar wireless sensor network (WSN, many small radio-linked sensor nodes) has run since 2009 with ~25 nodes. It uses piezometers (water-pressure sensors in soil), tilt meters and geophones (ground-vibration sensors) (sourced: [Amrita](https://www.amrita.edu/center/awna/research/landslide-monitoring/)). The Sikkim Chandmari network has more than 200 sensors over ~150 acres and cost **Rs 5 crore** (sourced: [Assam Tribune](https://assamtribune.com/sikkim-gets-real-time-landslide-warning-system)). IIT Mandi's low-cost node costs about **Rs 11,000 to install and Rs 5,000 a year** (sourced, press quote: [Tribune](https://m.tribuneindia.com/news/archive/himachal/archive-608331)). A route system should therefore rely on gridded rain for every segment. It should add sensors only at known hotspots, as an "override" input (inference).

Crowd risk follows the same logic, with density as the trigger. Flow (people per metre per second) rises with density until a **critical density of about 2–3 persons/m²**, then falls (sourced: [G. Keith Still](https://gkstill.com/Support/crowd-flow/fruin/Fruin1.html)). Helbing's analysis of the 2006 Hajj crush found laminar flow turned into stop-and-go waves, then into "crowd turbulence" (sourced: [arXiv physics/0701203](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0701203)). Deaths began about **30 minutes after stop-and-go started**, and turbulence is associated with densities above ~6 persons/m² (sourced, secondary: [Physics World](https://physicsworld.com/a/crowd-turbulence-has-deadly-consequences/)). That 30-minute window is the time that an approval workflow must fit inside (inference). On a narrow trail, RFID gate counts are a cheaper and more robust density signal than cameras. Occupancy = entries − exits between two readers, divided by segment area (inference). If you add a camera demo, density-map models (a CNN that outputs a per-pixel heat map whose sum is the head count) beat box detectors such as YOLO in dense scenes. CSRNet scores **MAE ≈ 68** (mean absolute error, the average miss in head count per image) on ShanghaiTech Part A (sourced: [arXiv 2004.03597](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.03597)). DM-Count is MIT-licensed and ships pretrained weights (sourced: [DM-Count GitHub](https://github.com/cvlab-stonybrook/DM-Count)).

The alert layer has a clear standard. CAP 1.2 is ITU-T X.1303bis. It defines `urgency`, `severity` and `certainty` fields, and it allows one `info` block per language (sourced: [ITU CAP 1.2 module](https://www.itu.int/wftp3/Public/t/fl/itu-t/x/x1303bis/2014/CAP-1-2.html)). WMO's open-source CAP Composer already models a **draft → review → publish** workflow (sourced: [CAP Composer docs](https://cap-composer.readthedocs.io/en/latest/)). The UN "Early Warnings for All" plan sets four pillars. They are risk knowledge, detection and forecasting, warning dissemination, and preparedness and response (sourced: [WMO Bulletin](https://wmo.int/resources/wmo-bulletin/wmo-bulletin-vol-72-1-2023/overview-of-early-warnings-all-executive-action-plan-2023-2027)). These four pillars make a ready-made structure for the deck (inference).

The three disasters make the gap concrete. At Amarnath on 8 Jul 2022, the cave station recorded **31 mm in two hours**. IMD said this was below the cloudburst threshold of 100 mm/h (sourced: [Onmanorama](https://onmanorama.com/news/india/2022/07/09/amarnath-flash-floods-not-caused-by-cloudburst-imd.html)). MoES said the upper reaches may have had more rain, "for which data are not available" (sourced: [MoES RS 1300](https://www.moes.gov.in/sites/default/files/RS-in-English-1300-28-07-2022.pdf)). At Chasoti on 14 Aug 2025, IMD had issued alerts for the Chenab valley region, but the yatra went on. About 61–64 people died. IMD's J&K director called compliance "very little or negligible" (sourced: [ETV Bharat](https://www.etvbharat.com/en/!bharat/kishtwar-cloudburst-exposes-laxity-in-response-to-weather-warnings-in-jammu-kashmir-enn25082003499)). At Adhkuwari on 26 Aug 2025, a landslide killed at least 34 people at 14:40. The Board says it closed the old track by 12:00. Critics say it ignored several alerts (sourced: [Tribune](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/j-k/no-lapses-vaishno-devi-board-calls-landslip-tragedy-unforeseeable)). The inquiry report has not been made public (sourced: [Tribune](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/j-k/deadline-over-vaishno-devi-landslip-probe-report-not-out-yet)). In each case the forecast reached some official. What failed was the step from "region alert" to "close segment X, clear the N people already on it, and record who decided" (inference).

So YatraKavach should **integrate, not duplicate**. Do not rebuild registration, RFID hardware, CCTV analytics, the SASB SOS app, IMD forecasting, GSI bulletins or SACHET distribution. Instead, take their outputs as inputs and add four things none of them shows in public (inference). The first is a risk score per ~500 m route segment. The second is fusion of that score with how many people are on the segment now. The third is a recommended action that a named officer must approve, with time stamps and escalation. The fourth is a CAP message that SACHET, an app or a camp PA (public-address speaker) could carry, in the pilgrims' languages.

## Build the scorer, approval loop and replay for real; simulate the feeds

The recommended design is one pipeline with five stages. It uses only the given stack (Python/FastAPI, PostgreSQL/PostGIS, Leaflet or MapLibre, Open-Meteo). The design itself is inference, built from the sourced patterns above.

```
OSM trail lines ──► split into ~500 m segments ──► PostGIS `segments`
                    (slope from DEM, susceptibility class, type: ledge/bridge/nala/camp)
                                     │
Open-Meteo (every 15 min) ──► rain features per segment (1 h, 24 h, 15-day sums)
Simulated RFID gate events ─► occupancy per segment (entries − exits)
                                     │
                         scoring engine (rules, explainable)
             hazard level × exposure → GREEN / YELLOW / ORANGE / RED
                                     │
          proposed action (hold at gate / stop ascent / clear segment)
                                     │
   officer console: APPROVE / EDIT / REJECT  →  append-only audit log
                                     │
     CAP 1.2 XML (status="Exercise") + multilingual text → simulated channels
```

**Segments.** Pull the trails from OpenStreetMap (OSM, the open map database) with the Overpass API (a query service for OSM data). Cut each line into ~500 m pieces with `shapely.ops.substring`. Store them as `LINESTRING` geometry in PostGIS (the spatial extension for PostgreSQL). Add slope from a 30 m DEM (digital elevation model, a grid of ground heights) such as Copernicus GLO-30. Try GSI susceptibility data from Bhukosh for one hour. If the download fails, use a slope-based proxy (inference). OSM completeness for the Amarnath and Machail trails was not checked, so expect to digitise some parts by hand (inference). Mark junctions, bridges and nala crossings as their own segments, because crushes happen where flows meet. The 2015 Mina crush happened where two flows met at right angles (sourced: [Foreign Policy](https://foreignpolicy.com/2015/09/24/the-deadliest-stop-on-the-road-on-mecca/)).

**Scale.** About 90 km of route at 500 m gives ~180 segments (estimate). Open-Meteo cells are ~9–11 km wide, so only ~15–25 unique grid points are needed (estimate). That is ~100 calls a day, far below the free limit of **10,000 calls/day for non-commercial use** (sourced: [Open-Meteo terms](https://open-meteo.com/en/terms)). Data are CC BY 4.0, so the map must show an Open-Meteo credit (sourced: [Open-Meteo licence](https://open-meteo.com/en/licence)). 180 segments × 96 updates/day ≈ 17,000 rows/day. Plain Postgres handles this easily, so a time-series DB (database tuned for timestamped data, such as TimescaleDB) is not needed (estimate).

**Scoring.** Use explainable rules, not a black-box model. Watch level: 24-hour rain above the Shah et al. daily threshold (14.35 mm Jammu side; 9.4 mm Kashmir side), or 15-day rain above 55 mm. Warning level: an I–D exceedance on a Himalayan curve (inference, thresholds sourced above). Multiply by a segment factor for slope, susceptibility and type. Then combine with crowd exposure. One option is: risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability. Escalate when the hazard is at least amber **and** occupancy is high (inference, following the impact-based approach in [EGUsphere 2025-4940](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2025/egusphere-2025-4940/egusphere-2025-4940.pdf)). Use configurable crowd bands: green < 2, amber 2–4, red 4–6, black ≥ 6 persons/m². The 2–3 and 6 values are sourced above; the 4 cut-off is an interpolation (inference). Add hysteresis (a higher bar to raise a level than to clear it) so the colours do not flicker (inference). Show the **drivers** on each segment, for example "24 h rain 38 mm = 2.6× threshold; slope 41°; 1,200 people on segment". Norway's xgeo and LANDSLIP both show operators which thresholds were crossed, not raw probabilities (inference from sourced designs).

**Honesty about 15 minutes.** Open-Meteo's 15-minute data are native only in Central Europe and North America. Elsewhere they are interpolated from hourly data (sourced: [Open-Meteo Historical Forecast API](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/historical-forecast-api)). Its models run at 9–13 km, and a cloudburst covers ~20–30 km². So the prototype cannot detect a cloudburst cell. Label the output "convective watch", not "cloudburst prediction" (inference). Say clearly that in deployment the trigger would be IMD radar or station data.

**Approval loop.** Model the alert as a state machine (a fixed set of states with allowed transitions): `PROPOSED → APPROVED → SENT → ACKED → CANCELLED/EXPIRED`. Require a second approver for "clear segment" or public broadcast. Allow a logged "break-glass" override (an emergency bypass with a written reason) so that approval never blocks a life-safety alert inside the ~30-minute window (inference). Each transition writes an append-only audit row. The row stores who acted, when, an input snapshot, the threshold version and the message text. If nobody approves within N minutes, escalate to the next officer (inference). This directly answers the "who decided" gap seen at Adhkuwari and Chasoti.

**CAP output.** Generate valid CAP 1.2 XML with `status="Exercise"` during demos. Use one `info` block per language and a polygon around the affected segments. Use `msgType` Alert/Update/Cancel so that corrections point to the original (sourced format: [ITU CAP 1.2](https://www.itu.int/wftp3/Public/t/fl/itu-t/x/x1303bis/2014/CAP-1-2.html)). Write messages as WHAT – WHERE (segment + landmark) – DO (one action) – UNTIL. Use pre-approved templates per language, not live machine translation. Real SMS in India needs TRAI DLT registration (a telecom registry of senders and message templates) (sourced: [MSG91 DLT help](https://msg91.com/help/dlt-registration-in-india)), so templates are needed anyway (inference).

**Replay mode.** This is the demo's centrepiece. Open-Meteo's Historical Forecast API holds archived forecasts (ECMWF IFS from 2017) (sourced: [Open-Meteo docs](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/historical-forecast-api)). Replay 10–28 Aug 2025 to show what the system would have proposed before Chasoti (14 Aug) and Adhkuwari (26 Aug) (inference). The research found no observed rain totals for those sites (sourced gap). So the replay could also show **no** early signal. Run it on day 1. If the model forecast missed the storms, say so on screen: "a 9 km forecast did not see this cell; this is why radar input and the human officer matter". That is still an honest and strong point (inference).

|---|---|---|
| Route segments, slope, type | Yes: OSM + DEM in PostGIS | Hand-digitised parts where OSM is missing |
| Rain trigger | Yes: live Open-Meteo, every 15 min | — |
| Aug 2025 replay | Yes: Historical Forecast API | — |
| Landslide susceptibility | Bhukosh GSI layer if it downloads | Slope proxy if not |
| Crowd occupancy | Scoring logic is real | RFID gate events from a Python generator |
| Camera density | Optional: DM-Count on one sample image | No live CCTV |
| Sensors (tilt, rain gauge) | — | MQTT (a light publish/subscribe IoT protocol) messages from a script, as "hotspot override" |

| CAP 1.2 XML | Yes: valid XML, `status=Exercise` | — |
| Delivery channels | Web dashboard; optionally WhatsApp Cloud API test number (up to 5 recipients) ([Meta docs](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/whatsapp/cloud-api/get-started/)) | SACHET, cell broadcast, sirens, PA, SMS: log + UI only |

A feasible 3-day order is as follows (estimate, assumes ~10 working hours a day). Day 1: segments, Open-Meteo ingest, scoring, and a first replay run. ## Conclusion

The research changes what "innovation" means for this entry. India does not lack forecasts, cameras or alert channels. It lacks a thin, accountable layer that joins them at the scale of a 500 m trail section and records who acted. That layer is mostly workflow and data modelling, which suits one developer far better than new sensors or a new ML model. It is also the part that existing government programmes and A.N.T.S are least likely to own.

The main risk is not technical. The Shah et al. thresholds will fire often. The 9 km forecast may miss the August 2025 storms. Most crowd and channel inputs will be simulated. An entry that shows these limits openly, and turns them into an argument for officer approval and radar integration, will look more credible to judges than one that claims prediction. Confirm the deadline today, then run the replay first. Its result decides whether the story is "we would have caught it" or "this is why the human loop matters".
